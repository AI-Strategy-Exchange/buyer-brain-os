# How to read a buyer

How people actually decide, and how to read that off a call. Each section says what to look for, why it matters, and what it hands the competitor read. None of it tells you what you will find. The calls do.

## Part 1: Reading one call

### The story of the switch
Nobody buys the moment a problem appears. There is a first crack in the old way, a stretch of passively living with it, an event that makes it urgent, then active looking. Four forces run the whole time: the push of the current situation, the pull of something better, anxiety about the new thing, and habit holding them to the old way. Pain alone does not move anyone; push and pull have to beat anxiety and habit.

Rebuild the timeline from the call: what they had, when it first cracked, what happened that made them take this call now, what they already tried. Then list the forces on each side in their words.

*For the competitor read:* does a page increase the pull, calm the anxiety, or make the old habit easy to leave? Or does it only restate the push they already feel?

### Past episodes over opinions
What people did last week is worth more than what they "usually" do, and far more than what they say they would do. Intentions predict behavior poorly. A workaround they pay for, a thing they built, a hire they made, an hour they lose every Monday: that is real.

Find the concrete moments ("last month we...", "yesterday I had to...") and weight them over generalities and hypotheticals.

*For the competitor read:* does the page's proof look like the buyer's actual episode, or like generic benefit copy?

### Whose words
People often cannot report why they want something; they give a plausible story. And a buyer agreeing with the seller's framing is not the buyer's framing. The honest lines are the ones they bring unprompted, and above all the moments they correct or reframe the seller.

For every important line, note the seller's question before it. Did the buyer volunteer this, echo it, or push back on it? When the seller supplied the consequence ("so that must cost you clients?") and the buyer said "yeah", the consequence is the seller's.

*For the competitor read:* test pages against the buyer's own vocabulary, not the category's.

### Every problem on its own, with its consequence
One person carries several problems. Some are friction they have made peace with; some cost them money, clients, time with family, standing. The difference is the consequence. How often something is mentioned is not how much it matters: one quiet line about reputation can outweigh ten complaints about tools.

Give each problem its own record. For each, find what it leads to in their world and what would make fixing it worth it to them. Note where two of their problems pull against each other.

*For the competitor read:* does a page connect to the consequence, or stop at naming the familiar problem?

### The ask and the need
The thing people ask for is usually a means to something they care about more. Many buyers arrive with the solution already picked, and the frame they picked it in may be wrong. Ask "and that would let you...?" of every request until you hit something they want for its own sake.

Record the ask exactly. Then write what the call suggests they need, and why you think so.

*For the competitor read:* most competitors answer the ask. The opening is usually in the need.

### Say versus do
Preferences are built in the moment, shaped by the question asked and what they were just comparing against. What someone says matters and what they do when something concrete comes up (a price, a date, a next step, bringing someone else in) often diverge. The divergence is information.

Find those moments. Say what you think the gap means.

### What they protect
Losses loom larger than equal gains. Behind most hesitation is something the buyer does not want to lose. And the current way, however clumsy it looks from outside, protects things: familiarity, feeling competent, control, cash, peace with the team, continuity with clients, freedom from blame.

Ask of every hesitation: what would they lose? Ask of every workaround: what is it protecting?

*For the competitor read:* compare a page against the current way on switching cost, learning curve, reversibility, and control, not only on the end state.

### Two kinds of stuck
A buyer who agrees they have the problem and still does not move is stuck in one of two ways. Either the current way feels good enough ("why change?"), or they are afraid of choosing wrong ("how do I know this is the safe one?", "I don't want this on me if it fails"). They need opposite things. Urgency helps the first and makes the second worse. More options and more information often make the second worse too.

Work out which one each buyer is in, and what makes the choice feel hard to them: unclear what they want, options they cannot compare, too much work to evaluate, or a downside they cannot undo.

*For the competitor read:* does the page prove change is worth it, or reduce the risk of picking this provider? Which stuck buyer is it written for?

### Walls and tradeoffs
People eliminate options that fail a single must-have, no matter what else they offer. Some objections are tradeoffs that more value can outweigh. Others are walls. Also separate no, not now, and only if.

Find the walls and who owns them. For "not now", find what would make it now.

*For the competitor read:* a page has to clear the walls before anything else on it is heard.

### Fast yes, fast no
Experienced people decide by recognition: this looks like the thing that burned me, this looks like people like me. They speak in compressed shorthand and react before they can explain. Those snap reactions carry a whole story.

Find where the buyer reacted fast, positively or negatively. What did it remind them of? What past experience are they pattern-matching to? Ask the seller what the shorthand meant if the call does not show it.

*For the competitor read:* what on a page would trigger that recognition, good or bad?

### The reason they have to retell
People choose what they can justify, to themselves and to others. There is almost always someone they will have to explain the decision to: a partner, a spouse, clients, a team, their future self. The reason has to survive that retelling.

Find who they answer to and what that person will ask. What reason would they give them?

*For the competitor read:* does a page hand the buyer words and evidence they can repeat to that person?

### Buying outside help
For owner-run businesses buying advice, services, or a build, needing help and being willing to buy it are separate. The need comes from growth goals and gaps they feel in their own capability. Willingness depends on whether they trust this particular outsider: will they understand my business, will I become dependent, is it worth the money and my time to implement, will I lose control. They often already lean on informal help (a friend, a peer group, their accountant, ChatGPT). Trust in the person, trust in the firm, and belief in the competence are different things, and rapport is not a buying signal. Services cannot be inspected before purchase, so buyers look for ways to see the process, the boundaries, and what happens if it goes wrong. A first-time buyer of this kind of help needs different reassurance than someone who has bought it before.

Find what triggers the need, what informal help they use now, what makes an outsider feel safe to them, and whether they have bought this kind of help before and how it went.

*For the competitor read:* does a page show it understands this kind of business specifically, make the work and its limits visible before purchase, and make the value and the effort concrete?

### What counts as proof to them
People differ in what convinces them: numbers, a story from someone like them, a peer who vouches, seeing it work on their own material, a trial they can walk away from.

Find what they asked to see, what convinced them before, and what they dismissed.

*For the competitor read:* does a page offer proof in their format?

### Their real world
People adapt to their actual environment, with its exceptions, tools, and people, not to the tidy version. Map what they do, what they think and feel while doing it, and where current support (tools, people, workarounds) fails them.

*For the competitor read:* does a page fit their real sequence of work, or only the abstract outcome in the headline?

### Where the language gets hot
Repetition, metaphors, hedges, and sudden emotion mark what matters to a speaker. They are places to dig, not conclusions. Read them with what came before and after.

### What happened in the room
A turn means something in response to the turn before it. Did the seller's answer actually address the concern, and did the buyer engage or go thin? If the seller talked most of the call, the "voice of the buyer" may just be short answers to the seller's questions. On calls with more than one buyer, who talks, who defers, and who decides are often different people.

## Part 2: Reading across calls

People behave differently depending on the situation, so describe patterns as "when this is happening, this is what they carry and need," not as personality types. A group average often describes nobody in the group.

- Read every call on its own first.
- Then look for the same underlying problem in different clothes: a founder, an ops lead, and a consultant can describe one problem in three vocabularies. Group by situation and what is at stake, not by shared nouns.
- Name as many patterns as the calls actually hold. One person can sit in several.
- A pattern that shows up often is not automatically the one that matters most. Weigh consequence, not only count.
- Keep the outliers. One person saying something sharply that nobody else says may be the opening every competitor missed.

## Part 3: What the card hands the competitor read

For each buyer pattern, the card should say what a page would need to do for this buyer to feel understood:
- the forces: what push they feel, what pull would move them, what anxiety and habit hold them
- which kind of stuck they are in
- the walls a page must clear first
- what they protect, and what their current way gives them
- the reason they would retell, and to whom
- what counts as proof to them
- for services: what would make this outsider feel safe
- their real alternative, in their words, which a page has to beat, not just other vendors
